# Reckonsolve Forecasting Rulebook

Reckonsolve is a personal forecasting journal. Its purpose is not to put probabilities on every goal, intention, plan, or future action in my life.

Its purpose is to help me develop **calibrated observational judgment under personally relevant uncertainty**: the ability to form, update, and evaluate probabilistic beliefs about outcomes that matter to me while remaining primarily a forecaster rather than an author of those outcomes.

The central boundary is:

> Reckonsolve is personal in relevance, but limited in causal control.

A question belongs in Reckonsolve only if it is either:
- An **observational personal forecast**
- A **policy-conditioned personal forecast**

Questions whose outcomes I can substantially steer after forecasting them are not Reckonsolve forecasts unless that influence can be cleanly constrained by a precommitted policy.

Questions about what Future Me will choose, accomplish, resist, or make happen may be useful for planning, self-knowledge, commitment, or behavior change. They belong to a different tool or practice.

---

## Principle 1 — Reckonsolve Trains Forecasting, Not Self-Control

### Rule 1.1 — Only two kinds of personal forecasts are admissible

#### Observational personal forecast

An **observational personal forecast** concerns an uncertain outcome that is personally relevant to me but over which, after committing the forecast, I have little meaningful discretionary control.

The basic posture is:

> Forecast → observe → resolve.

I may have helped create the situation before making the forecast. That does not disqualify the question.

What matters is that after the forecast is committed, I can mostly take my hands off the outcome and watch what happens.

Examples:
- What date will this package arrive?
- What score will I receive on an exam I have already taken?
- What amount will the mechanic quote after I have already authorized the diagnostic work?
- Will I receive an interview invitation within 14 days after submitting this application, assuming I do not contact the recruiter?
- How long will a laboratory take to post a test result after the sample has already been collected?

#### Policy-conditioned personal forecast

A **policy-conditioned personal forecast** concerns an outcome that I could meaningfully influence after forecasting, but only after I explicitly precommit to a sufficiently clear policy governing the relevant actions I will and will not take.

The basic posture is:

> Freeze policy → forecast under that policy → follow policy → observe → resolve.

For example:

> How many days will my friend take to RSVP if I send no reminders, prompts, indirect nudges, or requests through another person?

The forecast is not simply about RSVP time.

It is about RSVP time **under a specified intervention policy**.

The policy is part of the question's meaning because it helps define the process whose outcome I am trying to predict.

#### Inadmissible agentic forecast

An **agentic forecast** concerns an outcome that I can substantially steer after making the prediction.

Examples:
- Will I exercise tomorrow?
- Will I finish this book by Sunday?
- Will I save $500 this month?
- How many hours will I study tonight?
- Will I finish this project before Friday?
- Will I avoid ordering takeout this week?

These questions may reveal something about my habits, motivation, follow-through, or future behavior. They are not useless.

They are simply outside Reckonsolve's purpose because the same person is both forecasting the outcome and substantially producing it.

### Rule 1.2 — Personal means personally relevant, not necessarily about me

A Reckonsolve forecast does not need to predict my own behavior.

It can concern people, organizations, objects, processes, institutions, circumstances, or events that intersect with my life.

Examples:
- How long will a friend take to reply?
- What will a contractor quote me?
- Will a delayed flight leave before midnight?
- How long will a submitted application remain under review?
- How many days will a replacement part take to arrive?
- Will a restaurant still have reservations available tomorrow?

The important distinction is:

> A forecast can be deeply personal because I care about the outcome even when I do not control the outcome.

### Rule 1.3 — The score should mean something epistemic

A good Reckonsolve score should primarily tell me something about the quality of my probabilistic judgment.

It should not be a mixture of:
- How well I predicted the outcome
- How much effort I exerted afterward
- How strongly I wanted the prediction to come true
- How effectively I manipulated the outcome
- How much self-control I displayed

If those factors are inseparable from the result, the question is probably not a good Reckonsolve forecast.

### Rule 1.4 — Do not turn Reckonsolve into a commitment device by accident

Suppose I forecast:

> 90% chance I exercise tomorrow.

If I later exercise partly because I want the prediction to resolve Yes, the forecast has become entangled with behavior change.

The reverse can also happen.

Suppose I forecast:

> 10% chance I exercise tomorrow.

A desire to preserve predictive accuracy could perversely make exercise feel like "ruining" the prediction.

Reckonsolve should never create an incentive to make the world conform to a forecast.

> The purpose of a forecast is to describe uncertainty, not to become a promise that I must fulfill.

## Principle 2 — Substantial Control Determines Whether a Question Is Admissible

### Rule 2.1 — Define substantial control in practical rather than absolute terms

**Substantial control** is post-forecast discretionary leverage that gives me a realistic way to materially alter the probability distribution of the outcome.

This definition has three parts:
- **Post-forecast** means the relevant control remains available after I commit the prediction
- **Discretionary** means I can choose whether to exercise it
- **Material** means exercising it could plausibly change the outcome in a meaningful way

The mere existence of some theoretical causal influence does not automatically make a question agentic.

I can technically cancel a package.

That does not mean an ordinary forecast about its delivery date must be treated as a policy-conditioned experiment.

### Rule 2.2 — Apply the Material Leverage Test

Ask:

> Is there an action available to me after forecasting that could materially shift the outcome distribution?

If no, the question is probably observational.

If yes, continue to the Realistic Availability Test.

#### Example: exam grade

I have already taken and submitted the exam.

I may later be able to request a regrade, but I cannot rewrite the exam I already submitted.

A forecast of the initially awarded grade is therefore largely observational.

#### Example: unfinished personal project

I ask:

> Will I finish this project by Friday?

I can work extra hours, abandon other plans, ask for help, reduce the scope, or stop entirely.

Those actions have obvious material leverage.

The question is not observational.

### Rule 2.3 — Apply the Realistic Availability Test

Ask:

> Is the influential action something I might plausibly take during the ordinary course of events?

If no, the question may still count as observational.

If yes, continue to the Policy Separability Test.

This prevents the rulebook from becoming absurdly strict.

#### Example: package delivery

I could theoretically:
- Cancel the order
- Reroute the shipment
- Request a delivery hold

If I have no ordinary reason to do any of those things, they do not automatically transform a delivery-date forecast into Type 2.

The relevant question is not merely:

> Could I intervene?

It is:

> Is intervention realistically part of how this situation may unfold for me?

### Rule 2.4 — Apply the Policy Separability Test

Ask:

> Can I specify and follow a clear behavioral policy that sufficiently constrains my influence?

If yes, the question may qualify as policy-conditioned.

If no, it is inadmissible.

A usable intervention policy must be:
- Clear enough to understand before forecasting
- Behavioral rather than purely motivational
- Concrete enough to audit later
- Reasonable enough to follow without distorting ordinary life
- Narrow enough to constrain the relevant influence
- Broad enough that obvious loopholes do not defeat its purpose

### Rule 2.5 — Use the A/B/C decision rule

Classify candidate questions as follows.

#### A — Observational

I may possess theoretical causal influence, but no realistic ordinary post-forecast intervention gives me substantial leverage over the outcome.

Examples:
- Package delivery date
- Exam grade after submission
- Medical test turnaround after the sample is taken
- Flight arrival time
- Initial mechanic estimate after relevant diagnostic choices are already fixed

#### B — Policy-conditioned

I retain realistic substantial influence, but it can be cleanly and reasonably constrained by a precommitted behavioral policy.

Examples:
- Recruiter response time under a no-follow-up policy
- Friend response time under a no-second-message policy
- RSVP time under a clearly defined no-prompting policy
- Contractor response time under a no-reminder policy

#### C — Inadmissible

My influence cannot be cleanly separated from the process.

The required policy may also be too ambiguous, too intrusive, too difficult to audit, or too artificial to preserve without distorting ordinary life.

Examples:
- Whether I finish a book this week
- Whether I exercise tomorrow
- Whether I complete a project by a self-imposed date
- How much money I save this month
- A social forecast where ordinary ongoing interaction is so entangled with the outcome that no clean policy can separate observation from influence

### Rule 2.6 — Use this decision tree

The question you should ask yourself is:

> Do I have post-forecast actions that could materially affect the outcome?

If **no**:
> Classify it as A — observational.

If **yes**, ask:

> Are those actions realistically available or plausible during the normal course of events?

If **no**:
> It can still usually be treated as A — observational.

If **yes**, ask:

> Can I cleanly and reasonably freeze that influence with a behavioral policy?

If **yes**:
> Classify it as B — policy-conditioned.

If **no**:
> Classify it as C — inadmissible.

### Rule 2.7 — Context matters because Reckonsolve is a single-user tool

The same surface-level question can be A for me and B for someone else.

For example:

> How many minutes until our restaurant table is ready?

If I normally wait quietly after checking in, the question is mostly observational.

If I regularly negotiate with the host, switch seating preferences, ask for bar seating, leave after a threshold, or mention time pressure, those behaviors may give me realistic leverage over the result.

The rulebook should model **how I actually behave**, not some abstract average person.

## Principle 3 — Prefer Observational Forecasts Whenever Possible

### Rule 3.1 — Prefer uncertain consequences over undecided actions

A strong Reckonsolve pattern is:

> Make the decision first → forecast the uncertain consequences second.

This reduces the amount of agency still floating around after the forecast is committed.

#### Better

I authorize a mechanic to inspect a specific problem.

Then I forecast:

> What repair estimate will I receive?

#### Worse

I forecast the repair cost while still deciding which mechanic to use, what symptoms to mention, which diagnostic work to authorize, and whether to pursue the repair at all.

Too many important causal choices remain unresolved.

### Rule 3.2 — Freeze major discretionary choices before forecasting when practical

Before creating a question, ask:

> Are there important choices I should make first so that the forecast concerns what happens afterward?

Examples:
- Send the email first, then forecast response time
- Submit the application first, then forecast recruiter response
- Choose the mechanic and authorize the diagnostic work first, then forecast the estimate
- Book the flight first, then forecast delay or arrival
- Place the order first, then forecast delivery

This does not mean every possible decision must be frozen.

It means the major decisions that strongly shape the target outcome should be settled when practical.

### Rule 3.3 — Do not manufacture observationality by pretending agency does not exist

Calling a question "observational" does not make it observational.

If I know that I will realistically retain strong control over the outcome, I should not ignore that control merely because the question would be interesting to score.

The rulebook exists partly to prevent this kind of self-deception.

### Rule 3.4 — Do not make policies unnecessarily elaborate when the question is already observational

A package-delivery forecast does not need clauses banning every imaginable action I could take.

Do not write:

> I promise not to cancel, redirect, intercept, destroy, steal, return, or otherwise interfere with the package.

That is unnecessary if none of those actions is realistically part of the situation.

The rule is:

> Control only needs to be constrained when it is materially relevant and realistically available.

## Principle 4 — Policy-Conditioned Forecasts Must Freeze Agency Cleanly

### Rule 4.1 — Treat the intervention policy as part of the forecast

For a Type 2 forecast, the intervention policy is not an optional note.

It defines the behavioral condition under which the forecast is supposed to resolve.

For example:

> How many days until my friend RSVPs?

is different from:

> How many days until my friend RSVPs if I do not remind, prompt, nudge, mention the RSVP, ask another person to bring it up, or otherwise attempt to accelerate or delay the response?

The second question specifies a process.

That process is what I am forecasting.

### Rule 4.2 — Write policies in behavioral terms

A strong policy describes actions or omissions that can be observed later.

Good:

> I will not send another message about the event before they RSVP or the forecast deadline passes.

Good:

> I will not contact the recruiter through email, phone, LinkedIn, another employee, or any other channel before the forecast deadline.

Weak:

> I won't interfere.

Weak:

> I'll act naturally.

Weak:

> I won't do anything intended to influence the result.

The weaker versions depend too much on Future Me's interpretation of motive.

### Rule 4.3 — Prefer objective actions over subjective intentions

A policy should usually answer:

> What am I prohibited from doing?

rather than:

> What am I prohibited from intending?

This distinction matters because motives are easy to rationalize after the fact.

Suppose I send a message and later say:

> I wasn't *trying* to remind them. I just happened to mention the event.

That kind of reasoning creates unnecessary room for self-deception.

A better policy would define whether mentioning the event is allowed at all.

### Rule 4.4 — Define the relevant channels of influence

If influence can occur through several obvious routes, the policy should cover them.

For example, a no-follow-up recruiter policy might include:
- No email
- No phone call
- No LinkedIn message
- No asking a mutual contact to check
- No contacting another employee for the purpose of prompting action

Do not create loopholes that violate the spirit of the policy while technically preserving its wording.

### Rule 4.5 — Define allowed behavior when ordinary interaction may occur

Some Type 2 forecasts involve people I naturally interact with.

In those cases, a blanket "no contact" policy may be unnecessary or socially artificial.

A more useful policy might say:

> I will not bring up the RSVP or event myself. If they independently bring it up, I may answer normally. I will document any such interaction because it may affect the forecast.

This can preserve ordinary life while still constraining deliberate prompting.

However, this kind of question is more fragile than a clean no-contact experiment.

### Rule 4.6 — Distinguish clean Type 2 questions from fragile Type 2 questions

A **clean Type 2** question has a policy that is easy to state, follow, and audit.

Example:

> No contact with the recruiter before the 14-day deadline.

A **fragile Type 2** question has boundaries that interact with normal life in messy ways.

Example:

> I regularly talk to the friend whose RSVP time I am forecasting and the event may naturally arise in conversation.

Fragile Type 2 questions are not automatically forbidden.

They require more caution because:
- Ordinary interaction can become treatment
- It may be hard to determine whether a breach occurred
- The forecast itself may alter how I behave
- Post-hoc rationalization becomes easier

If the policy cannot remain crisp without making ordinary life unnatural, classify the question as C.

### Rule 4.7 — Do not require impossible passivity

A valid policy should not force me to distort ordinary life simply to keep a forecast pure.

Bad example:

> I will not mention vacations, money, calendars, summer plans, family visits, travel, schedules, or anything adjacent until my spouse raises the vacation question.

That may create a clean experiment only by making normal interaction absurd.

The rule is:

> A policy must constrain relevant intervention without turning ordinary life into a laboratory experiment.

### Rule 4.8 — Document policy-relevant interactions

If a permitted interaction occurs that could plausibly affect the outcome, document it in the prediction's history.

For example:

> Friend independently asked who else is attending. I answered normally. No reminder or RSVP request was made.

The purpose is not bureaucratic completeness.

The purpose is to preserve the actual context so Future Me does not later forget that the process changed in a potentially relevant way.

### Rule 4.9 — Keep the policy fixed once the forecast is committed

Do not quietly tighten or loosen the policy after seeing how events unfold.

If the original rule says:

> No direct reminders.

I cannot later decide that ordinary conversation also "obviously counted" as a reminder merely because the outcome became inconvenient.

Likewise, I cannot invent an exception after breaking the rule.

The original policy governs the original forecast.

Lessons learned should improve the next question, not rewrite the previous one.

## Principle 5 — Policy Breach Means Invalid, Not "Probably Fine"

### Rule 5.1 — A material policy breach invalidates a Type 2 forecast

If I materially violate the intervention policy, mark the prediction **Invalid**.

Do not:
- Score it anyway
- Add an asterisk and pretend comparability remains intact
- Decide that the intervention "probably didn't matter"
- Reinterpret the policy after seeing the result
- Preserve the forecast because I dislike losing a data point

The relevant principle is:

> Once the stipulated process changes materially, the original forecast no longer describes the process that produced the outcome.

### Rule 5.2 — Judge breach by the action, not by whether I can prove it changed the outcome

Suppose I forecast friend response time under a no-follow-up policy.

I send a reminder.

The friend still does not reply for three more days.

I should not reason:

> The reminder clearly made no difference, so the forecast is still valid.

The problem is not whether I can prove a causal effect after the fact.

The problem is that I violated the condition under which the forecast was made.

### Rule 5.3 — Define a material breach

A **material breach** is an action forbidden by the policy that could reasonably have altered the outcome or the probability distribution over outcomes.

Examples:
- Sending a forbidden reminder
- Calling a recruiter under a no-contact policy
- Asking a mutual friend to prompt someone
- Providing information the policy explicitly said would be withheld
- Changing a key decision that the forecast treated as fixed

Trivial or unrelated behavior is not a material breach merely because it occurred during the forecast period.

### Rule 5.4 — Invalidation protects the dataset

Invalidation is not a punishment.

It is a statement that:

> This outcome no longer cleanly answers the question I originally asked.

A willingness to invalidate forecasts makes the remaining scored data more interpretable.

The goal is not to maximize the number of resolved predictions.

The goal is to preserve the meaning of the predictions that are scored.

## Principle 6 — Real Life Takes Precedence Over Forecast Integrity

### Rule 6.1 — Never preserve a forecast at the expense of a real-world objective

If I have a good reason to intervene, I should intervene.

Examples:
- I really want the job and following up with the recruiter is sensible
- A delayed medical result worries me and I want to call the office
- A friend needs an important reminder
- A practical deadline now matters more than observing an unprompted response
- Safety, ethics, money, health, relationships, or responsibility give me a good reason to act

Then invalidate the forecast if the action materially breaches its policy.

### Rule 6.2 — Reckonsolve is subordinate to life

The principle is:

> My life does not exist to generate clean Reckonsolve data.

A forecasting experiment should never trap me into passivity when action is appropriate.

### Rule 6.3 — Treat invalidation due to sensible intervention as a successful process outcome

Suppose I forecast recruiter response time under no contact.

Seven days later, I decide the job matters enough that a follow-up is worthwhile.

I contact the recruiter and mark the forecast Invalid.

That is not a forecasting failure.

It is correct adherence to the rulebook:
- The original process changed
- Life took priority
- The dataset remained honest

### Rule 6.4 — Do not delay necessary action merely to get a resolution

A forecast should not create thoughts like:

> I should wait another day so I can see whether I was right.

If acting now is what I would otherwise choose, act now.

Protecting a score is not a legitimate reason to alter real-world behavior.

## Principle 7 — Guard Against Forecast Reactivity and Self-Deception

### Rule 7.1 — Apply the Forecast-Reactivity Test

**Forecast reactivity** occurs when making, recording, reviewing, or caring about a forecast can itself change the outcome.

Ask:

> Is merely making or repeatedly seeing this forecast reasonably likely to change what happens?

If yes, the question is suspect.

This concern is especially strong for forecasts about:
- My effort
- My habits
- My intentions
- My motivation
- My purchases
- My studying
- My exercise
- My productivity
- My deadlines
- My self-imposed goals

### Rule 7.2 — Exclude questions where the forecast can become a goal

A question should not belong in Reckonsolve if seeing the prediction is likely to turn the predicted outcome into a target.

Example:

> 40% chance I finish this chapter tonight.

If seeing 40% motivates me to prove the prediction wrong, the forecast has become an intervention.

### Rule 7.3 — Exclude questions where I can "play god" with the result

A useful warning test is:

> After committing this prediction, could I intentionally make the outcome happen or not happen in order to vindicate the forecast?

If yes, the question is usually outside Reckonsolve.

The problem exists even if I promise myself I will not consciously game the result.

Subtle behavior can still drift toward the prediction.

### Rule 7.4 — Self-deception matters more than adversarial gaming

Reckonsolve has one user.

The main risk is not cheating other people.

The main risk is quietly creating explanations that protect my self-image or score.

Examples:
- "That reminder didn't really count"
- "I was going to do that anyway"
- "That conversation probably had no effect"
- "I didn't technically ask them to reply"
- "I only worked extra because I felt like it"
- "The outcome was basically fixed already"

The rulebook should reduce the number of situations where those arguments are even necessary.

### Rule 7.5 — Prefer clean exclusion over elaborate rationalization

If I need a page of reasoning to explain why my influence "probably does not count", that is evidence that the question may not belong in Reckonsolve.

When causal roles are genuinely tangled, excluding the question is often the more honest choice.

## Principle 8 — Resolution Criteria Define the Outcome and the Policy Defines the Process

### Rule 8.1 — Keep these concepts conceptually separate

**Resolution criteria** answer:

> What exactly counts as the outcome and how will I determine it?

**Intervention policy** answers:

> What behavior from me is being held fixed while the outcome unfolds?

They are different concepts even if Reckonsolve currently stores both in the same text field.

### Rule 8.2 — For now, place Type 2 intervention policies inside Resolution Criteria

A separate Intervention Policy field is not required merely for documentation.

Until Reckonsolve needs to operate on intervention policies programmatically, use a clearly labeled subsection inside Resolution Criteria.

Example:

```text
Resolution criteria:
Resolve to the number of whole calendar days from confirmed invitation delivery until the invitee submits Yes, No, or Maybe.

Intervention policy:
I will not remind, prompt, nudge, mention the RSVP, ask another person to prompt them, or otherwise attempt to accelerate or delay their response before resolution.
```

This preserves the conceptual distinction without adding unnecessary UI friction.

### Rule 8.3 — Promote intervention policy to its own field only when the software needs semantic meaning

A dedicated field becomes justified if Reckonsolve eventually needs to:
- Mark a prediction explicitly as policy-conditioned
- Ask at resolution whether the policy was maintained
- Offer "Invalid — policy breached" as a structured resolution path
- Filter observational and policy-conditioned forecasts separately
- Compare calibration across the two types
- Display policy-conditioned questions differently
- Enforce or validate policy-related workflow

Until then:

> Conceptually separate, physically colocated.

### Rule 8.4 — Resolution criteria should define the observable outcome before forecasting

When relevant, specify:
- What variable or event is being measured
- When measurement begins
- When measurement ends
- The source of truth
- The unit
- How rounding works
- What counts as the event occurring
- What happens if the event does not occur within the question's defined horizon
- How ambiguous or exceptional cases resolve

The Forecast Deadline is a separate mandatory field with its own meaning. It defines when forecasting stops, not what factual outcome counts as resolution.

Do not rely on Future Me remembering what Past Me "obviously meant".

### Rule 8.5 — Prefer objective resolution sources

A strong resolution source leaves little room for motivated interpretation.

Examples:
- Tracking history from the carrier
- A timestamped RSVP system
- Official exam grade
- Invoice or written estimate
- Laboratory portal
- Email timestamp
- Calendar date
- Published official record

A weak resolution source depends heavily on my subjective recollection.

### Rule 8.6 — Do not retroactively redefine the question

Once the forecast is committed, do not change:
- What counts as success
- What counts as the outcome
- What the intervention policy means
- The unit
- The start point
- The deadline
- The source of truth

If the original question proves badly specified but remains resolvable under the original contract, resolve it according to the rules that actually existed.

If the quantity, unit, source of truth, intervention policy, or other scoring-critical definition would need to change materially, mark the original prediction **Invalid** and create a new prediction under the new definition.

Then write the next question better.

## Principle 9 — Update the Model Freely but Do Not Change the Experiment

### Rule 9.1 — New evidence should change the forecast when warranted

Updating a forecast is not a failure.

If I receive relevant new information while the question remains valid, I should revise the probability or numeric distribution.

Examples:
- The carrier changes the delivery estimate
- A recruiter posts an update about the hiring timeline
- Weather deteriorates before a flight
- A friend independently mentions being unusually busy
- A mechanic reports an unexpected diagnostic finding

The forecasting skill includes updating beliefs when evidence changes.

### Rule 9.2 — Distinguish information from intervention

The central question is:

> Did I merely learn something, or did I change the process that generates the outcome?

Learning something usually calls for an update.

Changing the stipulated process may invalidate the forecast.

#### Example: valid update

A friend independently says:

> Work is insane this week.

If the policy allowed ordinary conversation and I did not prompt the relevant behavior, this may be new evidence.

I can update my response-time forecast.

#### Example: policy breach

I text:

> Hey, did you see my invitation?

That is not merely new information.

It changes the process by directly prompting the outcome.

### Rule 9.3 — Hold the stipulated intervention policy fixed

A concise rule is:

> Update the model freely. Hold the intervention policy fixed.

If the policy itself changes materially, the original Type 2 forecast usually becomes Invalid.

### Rule 9.4 — Do not confuse forecast revision with question revision

A **forecast revision** changes what I believe about the same question.

A **question revision** changes what is being asked.

Examples of forecast revision:
- 60% → 75%
- Numeric forecast: 90% interval 3–20 days, median 8 days, 50% interval 5–12 days → 90% interval 4–24 days, median 11 days, 50% interval 7–15 days

Examples of question revision:
- Changing the deadline
- Changing the source of truth
- Changing what counts as a reply
- Changing the intervention policy
- Changing the measurement start

Reckonsolve should preserve the first as normal forecasting history.

The second changes the experiment and requires separate treatment.

## Principle 10 — Social Forecasts Require Extra Care

### Rule 10.1 — Treat ordinary social interaction as a possible source of intervention

Forecasting other people can look observational while remaining causally messy.

If I regularly interact with the person, my behavior may alter:
- What they know
- What they remember
- What they feel urgency about
- Whether they respond
- What decision they make
- When they make it

This does not automatically forbid social forecasts.

It means the intervention policy may need more care than a simple "I will not explicitly ask".

### Rule 10.2 — Do not assume "no prompting" is always enough

Consider:

> How long until my friend RSVPs?

Suppose I promise not to say:

> Please RSVP.

But during ordinary conversation I mention:
- Who else is attending
- That space is running out
- That I already made reservations
- That I am excited to see them
- That I need final numbers soon

Those statements may influence the RSVP without containing an explicit reminder.

If this kind of interaction is likely, either write clearer rules of engagement or classify the question as too fragile.

### Rule 10.3 — Allow natural replies only when the policy says so

A reasonable social policy can sometimes be:

> I will not bring up the event or RSVP myself. If the person independently raises the event, I may respond normally. Any such interaction will be documented.

This makes the allowed interaction part of the forecasted process.

It is more honest than pretending ordinary conversation has no effect.

### Rule 10.4 — Move fragile social questions to C when normal life cannot be separated cleanly

If preserving the forecast requires monitoring every sentence I say or behaving strangely around someone, the question has crossed the line.

The right answer may be:

> Interesting question, but not a Reckonsolve question.

## Principle 11 — Theoretical Control Is Not the Same as Relevant Control

### Rule 11.1 — Ignore remote interventions that are not realistically part of the scenario

Almost any outcome can be influenced in some contrived way.

Examples:
- I could cancel a package
- I could deliberately miss a flight
- I could withdraw an application
- I could refuse to open a message
- I could sabotage a reservation
- I could contact an office repeatedly

If these actions are not realistically part of how I would behave, they need not convert an otherwise observational forecast into Type 2.

### Rule 11.2 — Do not use hypothetical extremes to make every forecast conditional

The rulebook should preserve useful personal forecasting.

It should not demand intervention policies for every theoretical possibility.

The standard is practical:

> Would this form of control realistically matter during the forecast period?

### Rule 11.3 — Reclassify when circumstances change

A question that begins as observational can become intervention-relevant if circumstances change.

Example:

I forecast a laboratory result turnaround as observational.

After an unusual delay, I decide to call the office and ask them to expedite the result.

If that call could materially change the timing, I should consider whether the original forecast remains valid.

The answer depends on what exactly was forecast and whether an intervention policy was part of the question.

When in doubt, protect interpretability rather than force a score.

## Principle 12 — The Boundary Should Be Conservative but Not Puritanical

### Rule 12.1 — Do not require zero causal influence

Zero causal influence is unrealistic for personal forecasting.

I chose:
- Which person to invite
- How to word an email
- Which mechanic to use
- Which flight to book
- Which package to order
- Which application to submit

Those prior choices may affect the outcome.

That is acceptable.

The main concern is **substantial post-forecast discretionary control**.

### Rule 12.2 — Focus on control that remains after commitment

A useful distinction is:

> Past choices establish the situation. Post-forecast choices can contaminate the observation.

The question is not:

> Did I have any causal role in creating this situation?

The better question is:

> After I commit the forecast, can I still substantially steer the outcome?

### Rule 12.3 — Err slightly toward exclusion when causal roles remain ambiguous

Reckonsolve does not need to contain every interesting uncertain question.

When a question sits on the edge and no clean policy resolves the ambiguity, excluding it is acceptable.

The cost is losing one forecasting opportunity.

The benefit is preserving the meaning of the entire calibration record.

> Breadth is less important than knowing what a good Reckonsolve score actually means.

## Principle 13 — Use Worked Examples to Calibrate the Boundary

### Rule 13.1 — Treat examples as patterns rather than rigid precedents

The same question may classify differently depending on context.

Use the examples below to understand the reasoning, not as an inflexible lookup table.

| Scenario | Default classification | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Package delivery date after ordering | A | Cancellation or rerouting is technically possible but usually not a realistic intervention |
| Exam grade after the exam is submitted | A | The relevant performance is already fixed and the initial grade is largely out of my hands |
| Medical test turnaround after sample collection | A | Calling is possible but normal turnaround is mostly external |
| Flight arrival time after booking | A | I am primarily observing the travel process |
| Restaurant wait after checking in when I normally just wait | A | Theoretical influence exists but is not realistically part of my normal behavior |
| Recruiter response after submitting an application | B | Follow-up is plausible and may materially change the response process |
| Friend response time after sending a text | B | A second message or call is easy, normal, and potentially influential |
| RSVP time with a strict no-prompting policy | B | Influence exists but can sometimes be frozen by a clear policy |
| RSVP time with frequent natural interaction and unclear boundaries | Fragile B or C | Ordinary conversation may itself affect the outcome |
| Mechanic estimate after diagnostic choices are settled | A or B | Depends on whether meaningful post-forecast choices remain |
| Whether I finish a book this week | C | I can directly change my reading behavior |
| How many hours I will study tonight | C | Forecasting can become planning or motivation |
| Whether I exercise tomorrow | C | I can make the prediction true or false through my own action |
| Whether I save a target amount this month | C | The outcome is heavily determined by my future choices |
| Whether I finish my project by Friday | C | I remain both forecaster and primary producer of the outcome |

### Rule 13.2 — Understand why "How many hours will I spend finishing this book?" is usually inadmissible

The question is personally meaningful.

It is also highly reactive.

Merely tracking it may:
- Make the book more salient
- Encourage me to read more
- Encourage me to abandon it
- Make me compare behavior against the forecast
- Turn the prediction into a challenge or target

The uncertainty includes my future actions, and my exposure to the forecast may change those actions.

That makes it a poor core Reckonsolve question.

### Rule 13.3 — Understand why the RSVP example may be either B or C

A clean version might be:

> How many days until my friend RSVPs if I make no further contact about the invitation and do not ask anyone else to prompt them?

That can be B if the policy is realistic and easy to follow.

A messier version is one where I regularly see or speak with the person and the event naturally comes up.

If ordinary social interaction cannot be separated cleanly from intervention, classify the question as fragile B or C.

### Rule 13.4 — Understand why the recruiter example is a good Type 2 model

Question:

> Will I receive an interview invitation within 14 days?

Policy:

> I will not email, call, message, contact another employee, or ask a third party to prompt the recruiter before the deadline.

This is a strong Type 2 pattern because:
- The intervention is obvious
- The policy is behavioral
- Compliance is easy to audit
- The policy does not require bizarre behavior
- If I later decide following up is worthwhile, Rule 6 applies and I can invalidate the forecast

## Principle 14 — Use a Standard Admission Procedure Before Creating a Forecast

### Rule 14.1 — Start with the Personal Relevance Test

Ask:

> Is this outcome genuinely relevant or interesting to me?

If no, Reckonsolve probably does not need the question.

The point of personal forecasting is not to manufacture arbitrary calibration exercises.

### Rule 14.2 — Apply the Meaningful Uncertainty Test

Ask:

> Is there enough uncertainty here that making a forecast requires judgment?

Avoid trivial questions where the answer is already effectively known.

### Rule 14.3 — Apply the Resolvability Test

Ask:

> Will I eventually be able to determine the outcome using clear criteria?

If the answer depends mainly on vague memory or subjective reinterpretation, improve the resolution criteria before forecasting.

### Rule 14.4 — Apply the Forecaster–Actor Test

Ask:

> After committing this forecast, will I primarily observe the outcome or substantially choose the outcome?

If I will primarily observe, continue.

If I will substantially choose, the question is C unless a clean policy can constrain that influence.

### Rule 14.5 — Apply the Material Leverage Test

Ask:

> What actions could I take after forecasting that might materially change the result?

Write them down mentally or explicitly.

If there are none that matter, the question is likely A.

### Rule 14.6 — Apply the Realistic Availability Test

Ask:

> Which of those actions would I plausibly take in the ordinary course of events?

Ignore contrived possibilities that are not realistically part of the scenario.

### Rule 14.7 — Apply the Forecast-Reactivity Test

Ask:

> Could making, seeing, or caring about this forecast itself change my behavior enough to change the outcome?

If yes, be suspicious.

This often identifies hidden Type 3 questions.

### Rule 14.8 — Apply the Policy Separability Test when needed

If realistic substantial influence remains, ask:

> Can I freeze that influence with a policy that is clear, behavioral, auditable, and reasonable?

If yes, the question may be B.

If no, classify it as C.

### Rule 14.9 — Apply the Normal-Life Test

Ask:

> Would following this policy make me behave unnaturally or sacrifice something I would otherwise reasonably do?

If yes, the question may be too artificial for Reckonsolve.

### Rule 14.10 — Apply the Self-Deception Test

Ask:

> If this forecast resolves badly, can I already imagine myself arguing that some intervention "didn't really count"?

If yes, tighten the policy or reject the question.

This is one of the most useful warning signs.

### Rule 14.11 — Apply the Invalidation Willingness Test

Ask:

> If life gives me a good reason to break the policy, am I genuinely willing to mark the forecast Invalid rather than rationalize the breach?

If no, do not create the Type 2 forecast.

## Principle 15 — Write Questions So Future Me Can Understand What Past Me Intended

### Rule 15.1 — Use explicit question wording

Prefer:

> How many whole calendar days will pass between confirmed delivery of the invitation and the invitee's first submitted RSVP?

over:

> How long will they take?

The first version reduces interpretation later.

### Rule 15.2 — State relevant assumptions

If an assumption matters to the process, record it.

Examples:
- Under the current shipping method
- Under the current reservation
- After the application has already been submitted
- Without further contact from me
- Under the diagnostic scope already authorized
- Assuming no cancellation by the airline

Do not overload the question with irrelevant assumptions.

### Rule 15.3 — Separate assumptions from intervention policies

An assumption describes the environment or situation.

An intervention policy describes my own constrained behavior.

Example:

Assumption:
> The invitation was successfully delivered on September 6.

Intervention policy:
> I will not send reminders or otherwise prompt an RSVP.

Keep those ideas distinct even if both appear in Resolution Criteria.

### Rule 15.4 — Use plain language before technical language

The rulebook may use terms such as:
- Observational
- Policy-conditioned
- Intervention
- Forecast reactivity
- Substantial control

When writing actual prediction text, favor wording Future Me will immediately understand.

A technically perfect policy that is hard to parse is worse than a clear one.

## Principle 16 — Preserve the Meaning of the Long-Run Record

### Rule 16.1 — Remember what Reckonsolve is trying to train

The target skill is:

> Calibrated observational judgment under personally relevant uncertainty.

That includes:
- Estimating probabilities
- Estimating numeric uncertainty
- Using base rates
- Modeling other people and external processes
- Weighing evidence
- Recognizing overconfidence
- Updating beliefs when new information arrives
- Distinguishing what I know from what I merely hope
- Learning how uncertain my environment actually is

It does **not** primarily include:
- Self-discipline
- Goal completion
- Habit formation
- Personal commitment
- Motivation
- Productivity enforcement
- Making my own forecasts come true

Those may deserve another system.

### Rule 16.2 — Protect comparability across predictions

A dataset containing mostly observational and carefully policy-conditioned forecasts has a coherent interpretation.

A dataset mixing those with heavily agentic predictions becomes harder to understand.

A good score could then mean:
- Good judgment
- Strong follow-through
- Easy goals
- Aggressive behavior change
- Convenient self-fulfilling predictions
- Some mixture of all of them

The rulebook exists partly to prevent that interpretive collapse.

### Rule 16.3 — Prefer fewer clean forecasts over many ambiguous ones

Do not optimize for forecast count.

A smaller set of questions that genuinely train the intended skill is more valuable than a large archive of predictions whose outcomes I helped manufacture.

> Reckonsolve should optimize for epistemic clarity, not prediction volume.

## Principle 17 — Final Pre-Commit Checklist

### Rule 17.1 — Run this checklist before creating the prediction

#### Question quality

> Is the outcome personally relevant or genuinely interesting to me?

> Is there meaningful uncertainty?

> Is the outcome objectively resolvable?

> Are the start point, unit, and source of truth clear when relevant?

> Have I chosen a mandatory Forecast Deadline that represents an appropriate latest forecasting cutoff rather than merely my expected resolution time?

#### Agency

> After forecasting, can I mostly take my hands off the outcome?

> What post-forecast actions could materially change the result?

> Are those actions realistically available in the ordinary course of events?

#### Reactivity

> Could seeing this forecast change my own behavior enough to change the outcome?

> Could the forecast become a goal, challenge, promise, or commitment?

> Could I deliberately make the forecast look better by steering the outcome?

#### Policy

If substantial realistic influence remains:

> Can I state a behavioral intervention policy clearly?

> Can Future Me determine whether I complied?

> Does the policy cover the obvious channels of influence?

> Can I follow it without distorting ordinary life?

> Am I willing to invalidate the prediction if I materially breach it?

#### Self-deception

> Am I relying on vague words such as "normally", "naturally", "interfere", or "intentionally"?

> Can I already imagine loopholes or post-hoc arguments about whether my behavior counted?

> Would a skeptical Future Me understand exactly what Past Me committed to?

#### Classification

If the question survives the checklist, assign it mentally as:

> **A — Observational**

or:

> **B — Policy-conditioned**

If it cannot cleanly fit either category:

> **C — Do not put it in Reckonsolve.**

## Principle 18 — Design the Forecasting Window Before Committing

### Rule 18.1 — Every scored forecast must have a Forecast Deadline

The **Forecast Deadline** is the predetermined last instant at which the forecast may be revised or deliberately retained through a Forecast Review.

It is part of the forecasting commitment itself.

A scored Reckonsolve prediction should not exist without one.

The question you should ask yourself is:
> What is the latest point at which this uncertainty should still be live and I would still consider changing my forecast based on new information?

The Forecast Deadline should be chosen before the initial forecast is committed.

Once committed, it is fixed.

### Rule 18.2 — Distinguish Forecast Deadline from Expected Resolution

**Expected Resolution** answers:
> When do I currently think I will probably know the answer?

**Forecast Deadline** answers:
> What is the latest point at which I should still be allowed to update this forecast?

These dates or times may be close, but they have different purposes.

Expected Resolution is a planning estimate.

Forecast Deadline is part of the scoring contract.

#### Example: mechanic estimate

Suppose I visit a mechanic on September 6.

I expect to receive the estimate on September 7.

A reasonable setup might be:
> Expected Resolution: September 7 at 3:00 PM

> Forecast Deadline: September 9 at 6:00 PM

The first says when I think I will probably know the estimate.

The second gives the forecasting process a reasonable outer horizon in case ordinary delays or new information occur.

### Rule 18.3 — Choose an appropriate forecasting horizon rather than trying to perfectly predict resolution time

The Forecast Deadline is not another forecast that I am trying to score accurately.

Its purpose is to define a reasonable **forecasting horizon**.

A good horizon should capture most of the period during which:
- The outcome may still reasonably be unknown
- New information may still arrive
- Updating would still be meaningful
- The original question would still be the same question

The target is:
> An appropriate latest forecasting cutoff.

The target is not:
> The exact moment I think resolution will occur.

### Rule 18.4 — Do not choose a deadline that is unnecessarily short

A deadline that is too early can prevent legitimate updating.

Suppose I expect a mechanic to reply tomorrow and set the Forecast Deadline for tomorrow afternoon.

If the mechanic instead needs three more days and provides meaningful diagnostic information during that period, I will be unable to incorporate that information after the deadline.

That is not a reason to extend the deadline later.

It is feedback that I chose too narrow a forecasting horizon.

### Rule 18.5 — Do not choose a deadline that is unnecessarily long

A very distant deadline is not a harmless safety buffer.

For Binary trajectory scoring, if the question resolves much earlier, most of the predetermined scoring window becomes neutral truncation rather than actual forecasting time. That makes the resulting Trajectory Brier less informative about the judgment I actually exercised.

For v0.7 Numeric forecasts, there is no Numeric trajectory score or neutral-truncation contribution. The same overly distant deadline is still poor design because it leaves the forecast updateable long after the uncertainty would normally be live.

If I expect a mechanic's estimate tomorrow, choosing a Forecast Deadline three months away would usually be poor question design.

### Rule 18.6 — Treat the deadline as a Goldilocks choice

A useful rule is:
> The Forecast Deadline should be late enough to include plausible ordinary delay and meaningful updating, but not so late that the question remains artificially open long after its uncertainty would normally be live.

This is a judgment call.

The rulebook cannot provide one universal buffer because different forecasting processes operate on different timescales.

### Rule 18.7 — Forecast Deadline must be an exact timestamp

A date alone is often too coarse for personal forecasting.

Questions such as:
- How long until this person replies?
- What will the mechanic quote me?
- Will this event happen before tonight?
- What time will this delivery arrive?

may have meaningful forecasting horizons measured in hours rather than days.

The scoring model therefore requires a timezone-aware timestamp.

The interface may provide convenient defaults, but the underlying commitment should identify an exact cutoff.

### Rule 18.8 — The Forecast Deadline is immutable after commitment

Do not extend the deadline because resolution is taking longer than expected.

Do not shorten it because the outcome appears likely to resolve soon.

Do not move it because the current forecast looks especially good or bad.

Changing the scoring horizon after seeing how the question develops gives Future Me an opportunity to alter the weight assigned to earlier beliefs.

The rule is:
> Choose the window before observing the trajectory. Then live with the window.

### Rule 18.9 — If the outcome remains unknown after the deadline, lock and wait

Passing the Forecast Deadline does not invalidate the prediction.

It only ends forecasting.

If the outcome is still unknown:
- The prediction locks
- No further forecast revisions are accepted
- No further Forecast Reviews are accepted
- Journal activity may continue if Reckonsolve otherwise permits it
- The prediction remains pending factual resolution
- The outcome may be recorded later when it becomes known

The scoring window does not extend while I wait.

### Rule 18.10 — Early resolution does not move the deadline backward

If the outcome becomes known before the Forecast Deadline, forecasting stops because there is no longer genuine uncertainty.

The original Forecast Deadline remains part of the immutable forecasting contract.

For Binary trajectory scoring, it remains the endpoint of the predetermined scoring window and the post-resolution portion receives the Binary neutral-truncation treatment defined by the Binary scoring design.

For v0.7 Numeric forecasts, there is no post-resolution neutral contribution and no Numeric trajectory score. The Deadline still determines the latest possible forecasting cutoff, while the final scoring revision is the last valid Numeric revision before the earlier of effective resolution and the Deadline.

### Rule 18.11 — Do not preserve a forecast merely because its deadline has not arrived

A Forecast Deadline is the latest permissible forecasting instant.

It is not an obligation to keep a question unresolved until then.

If the resolution criteria are satisfied early, record the outcome.

If a Type 2 intervention policy is materially breached, mark the prediction Invalid.

If the question otherwise becomes invalid, do not keep it alive merely to preserve its scoring window.

### Rule 18.12 — Separate effective resolution time from the time I record it

The **effective resolution time** is the earliest defensible time at which the outcome became fixed and ascertainable according to the Resolution Criteria and source of truth.

The **recorded time** is when I actually entered that fact into Reckonsolve.

Those may differ.

#### Example: package delivery

The carrier records delivery at:
> September 10 at 2:13 PM.

I do not open Reckonsolve until:
> September 10 at 8:04 PM.

The effective resolution time is 2:13 PM.

The recorded time is 8:04 PM.

Scoring should use the effective resolution time, not the later recorded time. For Binary this determines the trajectory cutoff and any neutral truncation. For v0.7 Numeric it determines which revision is the final scoring revision. Procrastinating on data entry must not change the score.

### Rule 18.13 — Use objective timestamps when available

Prefer effective resolution times supported by the question's source of truth.

Examples:
- Carrier delivery timestamp
- Email receipt timestamp
- RSVP submission timestamp
- Published result timestamp
- Laboratory portal posting time
- Official event time

If exact time is genuinely unknowable, define a reasonable resolution convention before forecasting when that precision could matter.

### Rule 18.14 — Do not backdate forecast revisions

A forecast revision becomes effective when it is actually committed to Reckonsolve.

Suppose I realize at 8:00 PM that I have mentally believed 80% since noon.

I cannot enter an 80% revision and claim it was effective at noon.

The recorded trajectory should reflect the belief states I actually committed when I committed them.

This protects the historical record from hindsight.

### Rule 18.15 — Let poor horizon choices become feedback

Sometimes I will choose a Forecast Deadline that turns out to be too short or too long.

Do not repair the historical question by moving the cutoff.

Instead ask afterward:
- Did I misunderstand how long this process normally takes?
- Did I confuse Expected Resolution with Forecast Deadline?
- Did I use an excessively large safety buffer?
- Was there a natural information horizon I failed to identify?
- How should I set similar deadlines next time?

Choosing forecasting windows is itself part of good question design.

## Principle 19 — Numeric Forecasts Express a Five-Quantile Distribution

### Rule 19.1 — Treat a Numeric forecast as a probabilistic distribution, not a point estimate with an error bar

A v0.7 Numeric forecast consists of five committed quantiles:

\[
q_5,\ q_{25},\ q_{50},\ q_{75},\ q_{95}.
\]

The interface presents them in human terms as:
- A **90% central interval** from \(q_5\) to \(q_{95}\)
- The **median** \(q_{50}\)
- A **50% central interval** from \(q_{25}\) to \(q_{75}\)

These values describe uncertainty about one well-defined scalar quantity.

They are not five independent guesses.

### Rule 19.2 — Understand what central intervals mean

For an effectively continuous quantity, the 50% central interval:

\[
[q_{25},q_{75}]
\]

places approximately:
- 25% probability below the lower bound
- 50% inside
- 25% above the upper bound

The 90% central interval:

\[
[q_5,q_{95}]
\]

places approximately:
- 5% probability below the lower bound
- 90% inside
- 5% above the upper bound

For whole-number or otherwise discrete quantities, probability mass may sit exactly on an endpoint. In that case the closed interval can contain more than the nominal percentage. The quantile values remain valid; do not force a continuous interpretation where ties are real.

### Rule 19.3 — Treat the median as a probability balance point, not necessarily the "most likely" value

The median \(q_{50}\) divides the forecast distribution into lower and upper probability halves.

It need not equal:
- The mean
- The mode
- The geometric midpoint of either interval
- A symmetric "best guess"

Skew is allowed and often appropriate.

For example:

\[
q_{25}=70,\quad q_{50}=100,\quad q_{75}=200
\]

can be a coherent forecast.

### Rule 19.4 — Keep the five quantiles ordered but allow equality

A valid Numeric forecast requires:

\[
q_5\le q_{25}\le q_{50}\le q_{75}\le q_{95}.
\]

Equal quantiles are allowed.

They may be especially natural for whole-number outcomes or highly concentrated beliefs.

Do not manufacture tiny differences merely to make the values strictly increasing.

### Rule 19.5 — Use any elicitation process that helps, but do not let the software dictate a cognitive sequence

A useful default technique is:
> Think broadly about the 90% interval → locate the median → refine the 50% interval.

This can reduce anchoring on a central estimate and encourages explicit tail consideration.

It is guidance, not a mandatory workflow.

Reckonsolve standardizes **what** a valid Numeric forecast contains.

The rulebook may teach **how** to think about those values.

### Rule 19.6 — Revise the whole distribution when evidence changes

A Numeric ForecastRevision is a complete new statement of:

\[
q_5,\ q_{25},\ q_{50},\ q_{75},\ q_{95}.
\]

If only one quantile changes, the new revision still represents the complete five-quantile forecast that now stands.

Forecast Reviews mean:
> I deliberately reconsidered the current Numeric forecast and retained the same distribution.

A Review is not a ForecastRevision.

### Rule 19.7 — Do not choose a confidence level

The v0.7 Numeric model does not ask me to choose whether I am giving a 50%, 70%, 80%, or 90% interval.

Every new Numeric prediction uses the same fixed representation:
- 90% central interval
- Median
- 50% central interval

This consistency improves comparability and calibration learning.

Legacy Numeric predictions created under the earlier user-selected-confidence model remain historical records under that older contract.

### Rule 19.8 — Distinguish elicited quantiles from software interpolation

The five quantiles are my committed probabilistic judgments.

Any curve Reckonsolve draws between them is an **implied distribution** created for visualization.

For distinct quantiles of an effectively continuous quantity, Reckonsolve may connect the corresponding cumulative-probability anchors by simple interpolation.

For repeated quantiles or discrete quantities, the same numerical value can correspond to several quantile levels and therefore implies a jump rather than several contradictory exact CDF values.

The interpolation convention is not an additional belief I personally stated.

### Rule 19.9 — Treat WIS as the per-forecast Numeric score, not a universal cross-question scale

Resolved v0.7 Numeric forecasts are scored with Weighted Interval Score using:
- The median
- The 50% interval
- The 90% interval

Lower is better.

WIS retains the unit and scale of the target quantity.

Therefore a WIS of 10 dollars and a WIS of 10 days are not comparable, and even two dollar forecasts at radically different numerical scales should not automatically be treated as equivalent forecasting tasks.

Use WIS primarily to understand one resolved prediction and to compare the initial and final forecast of the same prediction.

### Rule 19.10 — Use long-run calibration to learn whether Numeric uncertainty is behaving as claimed

Across many resolved Numeric predictions, ask whether the stated quantiles and central intervals behave like the probabilities they represent.

For effectively continuous quantities:
- About 5% of outcomes should fall below \(q_5\)
- About 25% should fall below \(q_{25}\)
- About 50% should fall below \(q_{50}\)
- About 75% should fall below \(q_{75}\)
- About 95% should fall below \(q_{95}\)
- About 50% should fall inside \([q_{25},q_{75}]\)
- About 90% should fall inside \([q_5,q_{95}]\)

For whole-number quantities, ties at quantile values require discrete-aware interpretation rather than pretending those equalities must hold exactly.

The point is not to make every single interval contain the realized outcome.

A well-calibrated 90% interval should sometimes miss.

The long-run pattern is what matters.

## Principle 20 — When in Doubt, Protect the Role of Forecaster

### Rule 20.1 — Return to the simplest test

When all of the terminology becomes confusing, ask:

> Am I mostly trying to predict what will happen, or am I still in a position to make it happen?

If I am mostly predicting, Reckonsolve is probably the right place.

If I am mostly making, choosing, controlling, or committing, it probably belongs elsewhere.

### Rule 20.2 — Use the core identity as the tie-breaker

The final tie-breaker is:

> Reckonsolve is for observing uncertain parts of my world with calibrated judgment.

It is not a general tracker for every uncertain statement about my future.

### Rule 20.3 — Let the rulebook evolve without rewriting history

Future experience will reveal edge cases the current rules do not anticipate.

When that happens:
- Preserve how past forecasts were actually defined
- Improve the rulebook prospectively
- Add examples that clarify the new boundary
- Avoid retroactively judging old forecasts by rules that did not yet exist

The rulebook should become more precise over time.

Its purpose is not to create perfect philosophical categories.

Its purpose is to keep Reckonsolve pointed at the skill I actually want to develop.
